Oncology November 3, 2026

An AI puts a number on a child's brain tumour odds

a personal estimate instead of a risk group

Children with brain tumours get sorted into broad risk groups, and the group decides the treatment. A model called MANTIS, reported on November 3 in Nature Genetics by researchers led by Dana-Farber, tries to give each child their own number instead, for two tumour types: medulloblastoma and ependymoma. Doctors currently place these children into broad risk groups. MANTIS aims to give a personal estimate instead.

The AI does the prediction. A neural network takes the tumour’s DNA methylation profile, a test already used to help diagnose these tumours, plus copy-number changes, and learns from long-term patient follow-up. The ASCO Post reports a training and testing cohort of 2,786 patients and a concordance index (a score where 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect) of up to 0.80, reported as better than current clinical indicators. The team then used the model to simulate lower radiation doses: it suggested that about 60% of children with average-risk medulloblastoma might do as well with a lower dose to the brain and spine, which the data linked to better thinking and learning outcomes.

Why it matters: Radiation to a child's brain costs them later thinking and learning ability, so knowing which children can safely get less of it is worth a great deal.

The catch: MANTIS is a research model, not in use in clinics. The 0.80 accuracy figure is a best case with a wide margin, and the radiation finding comes from replaying past patients on a computer, not from a trial that actually gave children lower doses.

Sources: Dana-Farber news release (EurekAlert) · The ASCO Post

Discovery November 4, 2026

Claude helped solve a protein structure, mistakes and all

an AI doing the unglamorous middle of a project

Vaults are hollow protein shells, spotted in animal cells back in 1986, and nobody knows what they are for. On November 4 Anthropic's biology team said its Claude model, working with outside scientists, had gone hunting for their relatives among bacteria and found some. Vaults were discovered in 1986 and what they do is still unknown.

The AI did parts of the lab project as an AI agent. Claude searched a database of about 15 billion predicted proteins for vault relatives (found in 29 bacterial and four archaeal groups), designed DNA for producing them in E. coli, suggested a purification method that cut the time from about three days to about 90 minutes, and ran the software that turns cryo-EM images into a 3D structure. Of 44 candidates tested in a cell-free system, seven of 15 that were made as soluble protein formed vault-like particles, and cryo-EM confirmed four. The team solved one from a bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria): a shell of 96 protein copies, compared with 78 in a human vault.

Why it matters: This is one of the first detailed accounts of a general-purpose AI doing the unglamorous middle of a research project — searching, planning, troubleshooting — rather than one clever prediction.

The catch: A company report with a preprint, not peer reviewed, and Anthropic lists its own model's mistakes: a bad additive cost two weeks, and a skipped step produced a flawed map. The analysis also used hundreds of GPUs, more than most academic labs have. What vaults actually do is still unknown.

Sources: Anthropic research post · Mind and Machine Weekly

Protein October 27, 2026

Pinal designs a protein from a plain English description

typing the job and getting a candidate back

Type what you want a protein to do, and Pinal will try to design it. Researchers at Westlake University in China published the model in Nature. A user describes the job in plain language, such as breaking down PET plastic, and the model proposes a protein to do it.

The AI does the design in two steps. It is a foundation model with 16 billion parameters, trained on about 1.7 billion pairs of protein and text description, with structures predicted by AlphaFold. One module turns the text into candidate protein shapes, and a second chooses the amino acids for each shape, a form of de novo protein design. The team tested four functions in the lab: a fluorescent protein, a plastic-degrading enzyme, an alcohol-processing enzyme and a metabolic helper protein. One designed helper protein raised the output of a carbon-dioxide-fixing pathway in E. coli by 1.7 times versus the natural protein.

Why it matters: Describing what you want in a sentence and getting a protein design back removes the specialist step between an idea and a candidate.

The catch: The hit rates are low and uneven. For the glowing protein, 50,000 designs were generated, 29 were built and one worked; for a helper protein, 5 of 20 worked. The 1.7-fold gain is a single comparison, and the paper is paywalled, so these numbers come from secondary reports.

Sources: Scienmag (bioengineer.org) · SingularityHub · Nature paper (DOI)

Funding October 28, 2026

BioGeometry raises $100 million for AI drug design in Beijing

the design-and-test loop, now with a Beijing entrant

The design-and-robot-lab loop that US startups have been building now has a well-funded Beijing entrant. On October 28 BioGeometry, about four years old, announced a $100 million Series A extension led by China Life Capital, taking its total Series A past $100 million.

The company says it runs two AI models plus an agent. GeoFlow predicts the atomic structure of biological molecules and designs new ones, including antibodies, from scratch. GeoCell is a virtual cell that predicts how cells respond to genes or drugs, to help choose targets and patients. An AI agent links both to an automated lab, so experimental results feed back into the models. The company says its third GeoFlow version averaged a 20% hit rate across more than 20 targets and found leads within three weeks, and that several bispecific antibody programmes should reach an IND filing in 2027.

Why it matters: China now has well-funded companies building the same design-and-robot-lab loop as the US leaders, and this one says it will file its first drug applications in 2027.

The catch: Every figure is the company's own. "Hit rate" is never defined, the comparison to AlphaFold 3 gives no benchmark, there is no clinical data, and the 2027 filings are projections.

Sources: Ventureburn · Endpoints News (headline only; paywalled)

Funding November 6, 2026

Roche signs Chai Discovery, the third big pharma this year

three giants, one small company, twelve months

Three of the world's largest drugmakers have now signed with the same small San Francisco company inside a year. On November 6 Chai Discovery said Roche would use its AI models across a number of drug discovery programs, following Pfizer in June and GSK in October. Chai says Roche follows Pfizer in June and GSK in October as partners this year.

The AI does the molecular design. Chai’s latest model, Chai-3, is described by the company as achieving high target success rates and binding affinity, including to challenging targets, for protein-based drugs.

Why it matters: Three of the world's biggest drugmakers signed with the same small AI company this year, which says they no longer treat designing proteins by computer as experimental.

The catch: No financial terms, no number of programmes, no timelines and no benchmark figures. The performance claims are Chai's own, and no drug from this has been tested in anyone.

Sources: Chai Discovery press release (BioSpace)

Discovery October 29, 2026

Flagship merges three biotechs to chase causes, not correlations

betting that better targets mean fewer failures

Most drugs fail because the target was wrong, and Flagship Pioneering is reorganising around that problem. On October 29 the venture firm merged three of its companies, Cellarity, Etiome and Empress Therapeutics, into one: Etcelera, based in Somerville, Massachusetts. It aims to find drug targets and small-molecule medicines with a better chance of clinical success.

The AI does the cause-and-effect analysis. Etiome contributes a machine-learning engine that combines several kinds of molecular measurement, single-cell data and electronic health records to track how disease changes over time. Empress contributes a platform linking genetic sequences to the small molecules cells make. Flagship says it combines these with its own AI to model causes in disease rather than correlations.

Why it matters: Most drugs fail because the target was wrong, and this company is betting that AI can tell a real cause of disease from a coincidence.

The catch: No funding, investors or terms were disclosed, and the claim of better clinical odds comes with no data. The only drug mentioned is in early testing. Merging three companies is a restructuring, not a result.

Sources: pharmaphorum · Endpoints News (headline only; paywalled)
