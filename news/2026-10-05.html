Protein design October 2, 2026

Xaira unveils the AI model it built to design antibodies, and two candidates made with it

Xaira Therapeutics, an AI drug company that launched in 2024 with more than $1 billion in funding, has described for the first time its antibody-design model, X-Design, and two of its early drug candidates. Antibodies are proteins that grab a specific target, and many medicines are built from them; finding one that binds a difficult target and is also stable enough to manufacture normally takes repeated rounds of lab screening.

The AI does the work in X-Design, a generative model that proposes antibody sequences on a computer. Chief executive Marc Tessier-Lavigne says the goal is to design antibodies that are "drug-ready from the start." The first candidate, XA-1 for cancer, was designed entirely by computer in seven weeks against an undisclosed target where earlier antibody attempts had failed. The second, XA-4, aims at a GPCR, a type of cell-surface receptor that has been hard for antibodies to target.

Why it matters: Xaira has not named either target and has given no timeline for human trials, and in the coverage available, no laboratory data were shown. The seven-week design time and the claim of "drug-ready" antibodies therefore cannot yet be checked from the outside. The company also declined to say how much of its original $1 billion remains. Treat this as a platform announcement, not a result.

Sources: Fierce Biotech

Discovery October 1, 2026

Talus Bio releases an AI model that predicts drug binding without needing a protein's 3D structure

Talus Bio, a biotech company, has released Ptarmigan-1, an AI model that predicts which small-molecule drugs will bind to which human proteins, without needing a 3D structure of the protein. Most computer screening of drug candidates fits molecules into a known 3D shape, which works poorly for proteins that are flexible or disordered, such as many transcription factors. Talus says these hard-to-shape proteins make up about 40% of the human proteome that was previously considered undruggable.

The AI does the work by learning from measurements Talus collects with its MARMOT platform, which tests how proteins behave inside living human cells, rather than from structures. Talus says this makes the model 5,000 times faster than structure-based methods, enough to screen billions of compounds a day. On a recently disclosed set of inhibitors for STAT6, a target in inflammatory disease, the company says the model picked successful candidates better than structure-based methods, even though it had never seen that target or those molecules in training.

Why it matters: The evidence so far is one disclosed target and the company's own report, and the "40% of the proteome" figure is Talus's estimate, not an independent one. A model that ranks molecules well is not yet a drug: any candidate would still need testing in cells, animals, and then people. Access is limited to a public portal, with wider use through direct collaboration, so outside groups cannot easily test the claim.

Sources: Talus Bio (BioSpace)

Protein design September 30, 2026

Google DeepMind adds invisible watermarks to AI-designed proteins

Google DeepMind has published a method in Nature for hiding a detectable watermark inside proteins designed by AI, so that they can later be identified as AI-made without losing their function. The aim is biosecurity: as AI tools make protein design easier, DNA synthesis companies and public databases have no reliable way to tell where a design came from.

The AI does the work in two places. For sequences, the team adapted the watermarking used for AI text to ProteinMPNN, a protein design tool, so that its choices of amino acids carry a statistical pattern a detector can find. For structures, they fine-tuned AlphaFold 3 so its predicted structures carry a signature. In lab tests on protein binders for three targets (the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, VEGF-A, and PD-L1), watermarked designs matched unmarked ones in binding affinity, and the structure watermark was detected with over 99.8% accuracy at a 0.1% false-positive rate.

Why it matters: The paper lists real limits. The sequence watermark can be removed by re-sequencing the protein, at a cost to its function, and it shows only that a design was marked, not who made it. It also marks only designs from tools that adopt it, so it does not stop someone using an unmarked tool. It is a provenance layer, not a safeguard on its own.

Sources: Nature · Google DeepMind

Oncology October 4, 2026

AI-designed molecules that destroy a blood cancer protein are described in a chemistry journal

Researchers at Insilico Medicine's Abu Dhabi site have described AI-designed molecules that destroy BTK, a protein that blood cancer cells need to grow, in the Journal of Medicinal Chemistry (published online August 14, 2026, and reported in UAE news on October 4). The molecules are PROTACs: two-ended molecules that tag a protein for the cell's own disposal system, rather than only blocking it.

The AI does the work in Insilico's Chemistry42 platform, a generative model that designed new chemical groups for gripping cereblon, a part of the cell's disposal machinery, and helped optimize the linkers joining the two ends across more than 40 compounds. The two lead molecules were tested in malignant B cells and in mice; in mice, about a quarter of an oral dose reached the bloodstream (26% and 29%).

Why it matters: These are research-stage compounds with no human data, and the authors say further preclinical and clinical studies are needed. Insilico says its AI cut development to 12–18 months from the usual 2.5–4 years; that is the company's figure for its own process, not a measured comparison. The reported results are from cells and mice only.

Sources: The National · ecancer · Journal of Medicinal Chemistry
