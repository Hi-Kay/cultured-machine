Protein design August 27, 2026

Generate Biomedicines' AI-designed antibody shows early promise in COPD and severe asthma

Generate Biomedicines' clinical data for golukibart (also called GB-0895), an antibody designed by generative AI to block a signal called TSLP that helps trigger asthma and COPD flare-ups, became public early after conference organizers accidentally posted draft copies of its research posters online nearly two weeks before the embargo lifted. In an ongoing Phase 1b trial in 40 people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a single dose produced "rapid and sustained reductions" across four disease biomarkers, with anti-inflammatory effects lasting at least six months in patients with high blood eosinophil counts; a companion Phase 1 trial in severe asthma showed similarly durable effects.

The AI does the work in how golukibart itself was built: Generate's generative model, Chroma, invents new protein structures and sequences directly — rather than starting from an existing antibody and tweaking it — letting the company set design goals like binding strength, a longer half-life, and manufacturability from the very start. The company says the result binds TSLP roughly 20 times more tightly than tezepelumab (Tezspire), the first approved TSLP-blocking antibody, while carrying antibody-engineering changes intended to keep it circulating in the body long enough for dosing just twice a year instead of monthly.

Why it matters: This is a Phase 1 trial in 40 patients, not the large, controlled Phase 3 studies that will determine whether golukibart actually helps people breathe easier or avoid hospital visits — and the data reached the public through an accidental leak, not a full company disclosure, so the usual context and caveats are still to come. Generate's stock swung several dollars in the days around the leak, a reminder of how much investors are already pricing in for AI-designed biologics that haven't yet reached late-stage trials.

Sources: BioSpace · Generate Biomedicines

Foundation models September 3, 2026

Nobel laureate David Baker launches a $95 million bet to teach AI biology's design rules

The Allen Institute, University of Washington, and Fred Hutch Cancer Center launched AI BioDesign, a five-year, $95 million initiative led by 2024 Nobel Chemistry laureate David Baker and genome scientist Jay Shendure, funded almost entirely by the Fund for Science and Technology set up from Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen's estate. The initiative pairs bench scientists with machine-learning teams inside one lab in Seattle, rather than training a single broad, general-purpose biology model the way many AI-for-science efforts do.

The AI does the work by learning design rules directly from data the lab generates itself: a DNA sequencer can read millions of designed DNA sequences from a single test tube at once, reporting which ones actually worked as intended, and a dedicated team of machine-learning specialists uses each batch of results to decide which experiment will teach the models the most before the next round begins. The goal, in Baker's words, is to shift biology's question from "what has nature already made?" to "what else is possible, and how can we test it?" — training AI on purpose-built experimental data rather than inferring design rules from the comparatively small set of proteins and genes evolution happened to produce. All results will be released publicly, free for others to build on.

Why it matters: This is an infrastructure and funding announcement, not a drug or a finished model — AI BioDesign has no product yet, and its own leaders say plainly that "the jury's still out" on whether narrow, purpose-built models like theirs will beat the field's dominant approach of one broad foundation model for all of biology. The institute says it needs to show real progress within 18 to 24 months.

Sources: GeekWire · UW Medicine

Discovery September 2, 2026

AI-designed protein shapes create RNA delivery vehicles that beat lipid nanoparticles in early tests

Researchers at Helmholtz Munich and the Technical University of Munich built a new class of RNA delivery vehicles, called Synthetic Transfer Vehicles, by combining natural protein building blocks with an entirely artificial structural protein. The best of more than 100 variants tested, STV-C8, delivered RNA into cells in culture far more efficiently than the lipid nanoparticles and virus-like particles the team compared it against, needing substantially less RNA to produce the same amount of protein.

The AI does the work in the vehicle's scaffold: a generative AI model designed the structural protein from scratch, and it settled on a geometry that does not occur in any natural virus — the researchers aimed to invent a new shape for the specific job of carrying RNA, rather than copy one nature already built. In mice, an intravenous dose of STV-C8 expressed its RNA cargo mainly in the lungs with no signs of toxicity, and when loaded with CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing components and injected into pig muscle, it successfully cut out a disease-linked section of the gene affected in Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

Why it matters: STV-C8 is still an experimental system tested only in cell culture, mice, and a single pig experiment — the researchers still need to work out how to direct it to specific cell types beyond the lungs and confirm it is safe and effective before it can be tried in people. The team plans to spin the technology out into a company, which is itself worth watching given how many RNA-delivery claims never make it past early animal data.

Sources: Helmholtz Munich · Nature

Funding August 26, 2026

Clinical-trial AI startup Faro raises $37 million as pharma pushes for agents that reason, not just draft

Faro, a startup building AI tools for clinical trial design, raised a $37.3 million Series B co-led by Merck's Global Health Innovation Fund and life-sciences investor S32, adding new investor Ankona Capital alongside its existing backers. The company says its software is already used by six of the world's ten largest pharmaceutical companies to design studies, spot risks, and manage the many interlocking decisions — from eligibility criteria to regulatory paperwork — that a single clinical trial protocol touches.

The AI does the work through what Faro calls a structured clinical-development data model: rather than treating a trial protocol as a document for an AI agent to summarize or draft, Faro's software converts a study's scientific intent, medical logic, regulatory requirements, and operational constraints into machine-readable form. That lets AI agents reason across a trial's design the way a person would — tracing how changing one endpoint affects the schedule of activities, the data that needs collecting, and downstream regulatory filings — rather than just generating text for a single task.

Why it matters: Faro is a private company reporting its own usage figures, and "understanding intent" is a harder, less externally verifiable claim than the document-drafting speedups most clinical-trial AI tools promise today. The company itself acknowledges that the shift to fully agent-run development workflows "will not happen all at once."

Sources: Faro · Endpoints News

Reality check September 1, 2026

Scientists mutated nearly every letter of a virus's genome — and AI still can't explain most of it

Researchers led by Ben Lehner at the Wellcome Sanger Institute created more than 44,000 variants of the bacteriophage ΦX174 — one of the most exhaustively studied viruses in biology — by systematically changing nearly every individual DNA letter and amino acid in its genome, then measured how each change affected the virus's ability to survive and multiply inside E. coli. About half of the single-letter DNA changes, and 60% of the amino-acid changes, turned out to be harmful, a far higher share than the researchers expected, while a handful of mutations made the decades-studied virus fitter still.

The AI does the work — or was supposed to: the team tested whether today's leading AI biology models, the kind increasingly used to predict which mutations matter in human disease and to help design new proteins and genomes, could predict the effects of these changes ahead of time. According to Lehner, the models "don't do badly, but they're not brilliant" — on this well-studied, comparatively simple genome, nearly all of them failed to beat a very basic statistical model, and for about a quarter of the harmful mutations, neither the AI models nor the researchers could explain why the change hurt the virus at all.

Why it matters: ΦX174 is a small, extraordinarily well-characterized system — the first genome ever sequenced and the first ever chemically synthesized — so if AI models struggle to predict mutation effects here, that is a meaningful caution about applying the same tools to far more complex human genomes and proteins. Lehner's team argues the shortfall traces to a lack of large, systematic experimental datasets to train on, not necessarily a flaw in the AI approaches themselves.

Sources: Nature · bioRxiv preprint
