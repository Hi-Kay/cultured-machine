Protein design July 29, 2026

An AI "shrink ray" can resize existing proteins without breaking them

A Duke University team built an AI tool called Raygun that shrinks or enlarges a natural protein — adding, deleting, or swapping pieces of it — while keeping its 3D shape and function intact. That is a different job from tools like AlphaFold, which mostly design new proteins from scratch; Raygun instead edits proteins that already work, which is often more useful for turning a lab tool into something practical.

The AI does the work through ESM-2, a protein language model that has learned the evolutionary patterns linking a protein's sequence to its shape (see our explainer on protein language models). Raygun translates a protein into a standardized numerical representation the model can resize predictably, rather than simply cutting or padding the amino-acid list. In lab tests, the team shrank two common fluorescent marker proteins by 10–25% — in some cases over 50% — and confirmed under a microscope that they still glowed.

Why it matters: This is a general-purpose editing tool, not a finished product — the DNA that has been synthesized and tested so far is limited to a handful of marker proteins. But a University of California San Diego student used the publicly posted method to design a protein that bound an EGFR cancer-signaling target better than the natural molecule it was based on, without prompting from the Duke team, which is a real signal that the tool generalizes beyond its own demo.

Sources: Nature (DOI) · Duke University School of Medicine

Oncology July 27, 2026

The FDA approved a blood test that uses AI to flag colorectal cancer

The FDA approved SimpleScreen CRC, a blood-based screening test for colorectal cancer in adults 45 and older at average risk, developed by Freenome and set to be commercialized by Abbott this fall. The test reads DNA methylation patterns in cell-free DNA circulating in the blood — chemical marks on DNA that shift in characteristic ways near a tumor — from a simple blood draw rather than a stool sample or colonoscopy prep.

The AI does the work in the classification step: an AI/machine-learning algorithm built into the test's software weighs the methylation and sequencing signal from each sample and calls it positive or negative, a pattern too complex to score with a simple threshold rule. In the PREEMPT CRC validation study, the largest prospective trial of its kind, the test caught 81.1% of colorectal cancers and correctly cleared 90.4% of people without advanced disease.

Why it matters: A blood draw is a much lower barrier than a colonoscopy or stool kit, so a test like this mainly helps if it gets more people screened at all — but it is markedly less sensitive than colonoscopy for catching precancerous polyps (13.7%), so it is a screening option to raise overall participation, not a replacement for follow-up colonoscopy when it flags positive.

Sources: Freenome · MedTech Dive

Funding July 30, 2026

GSK is paying $110 million for lab data, not an AI model

GSK expanded its partnership with Relation Therapeutics in a deal worth up to $110 million, but the structure is unusual: GSK is not licensing Relation's AI, it is paying Relation to manufacture the biological data that AI needs. Relation will run automated lab experiments at scale to generate large-scale human cellular data — measuring how cells respond to genetic and drug perturbations across immunology, fibrosis, and osteoarthritis — to train MORGAN, Relation's foundation model of cellular response.

The bet is that AI models for predicting how a human cell reacts to a drug or gene edit are limited less by algorithms than by the scarcity of large, consistent, machine-learning-ready lab data, since the public datasets available were mostly generated before anyone was training AI on them.

Why it matters: No cellular foundation model — MORGAN included — has yet produced an approved drug or even a clinical candidate, and a 2025 Nature Methods study found today's cellular foundation models perform no better than much simpler models at predicting how cells respond to perturbation; Relation's bet is that better, not just more, data changes that, which remains unproven.

Sources: BioSpace (press release) · Endpoints News

Discovery August 10, 2026

Novo Nordisk made Amazon's cloud arm its strategic AI partner for drug discovery

Novo Nordisk named AWS its preferred cloud provider and strategic AI partner, opening a joint "co-innovation hub" in London where AWS engineers and Novo Nordisk scientists work side by side. The AI does the work through two AWS products: Amazon Bio Discovery, which gives scientists direct access to AI models for generating and screening candidate drug molecules and links those predictions to physical lab testing, and Bedrock AgentCore, a platform for building AI agents that can run multi-step research and operations workflows across Novo Nordisk's data.

The companies point to an existing deployment as a track record: more than 25,000 Novo Nordisk employees already use an AWS-built generative AI tool for drafting documents and retrieving information, which the companies say has measurably cut clinical documentation time.

Why it matters: Neither company disclosed financial terms, a timeline, or any specific drug program tied to the deal, so this is an infrastructure and tooling bet rather than a result — the real test is whether Amazon Bio Discovery's screening actually shortens the target-to-first-dose timeline Novo Nordisk is aiming for, which won't be visible for years.

Sources: Novo Nordisk (press release) · AWS

Discovery July 27, 2026

Schrödinger built an AI "co-scientist" to run its own drug-design software

Schrödinger launched early access to Bunsen, an AI agent designed to plan and run computational chemistry workflows on the company's physics-based molecular modeling platform, with Bristol Myers Squibb as an early adopter. Rather than a general chatbot that summarizes existing knowledge, Bunsen interprets a research question in plain language, chooses which of Schrödinger's validated physics-based simulation methods to run, executes the multi-step calculation, and then explains the results back to the scientist — work that previously required a trained computational chemist to plan and supervise by hand.

The company frames this as widening who can use its platform: a "drug hunter" without computational chemistry training could, in principle, ask Bunsen to evaluate a set of candidate molecules and get back a prioritized, physics-grounded answer.

Why it matters: Bunsen is still an early-access product with no independent, published data yet on how often its recommendations hold up against expert-run analysis — this is Schrödinger's own three-decade-old simulation methods getting an AI planner in front of them, not a new discovery method in itself, and its real value will show up in whether customers' hit rates actually improve.

Sources: BioSpace (press release)
