Discovery October 27, 2026

Anthropic bought a one-person rare-disease biotech and reports early results from drug-development work with six partners

Anthropic, the maker of the Claude AI model, announced on October 27 that it has acquired Mithril Medicine, a one-person company developing treatments for two rare forms of muscular dystrophy. It also published early results from months of work with six outside drug developers. Almost all of the work is preclinical, meaning it has not reached human testing.

The AI does a range of jobs, not one. At Mithril, Claude organized contracts, logistics and experiments across 11 outside laboratories. At Divergence Labs, it helped generate about 35,000 antibody designs in roughly a month for a food-allergy drug. At Generate Biomedicines, it helped design a CAR T-cell component about 70% shorter than the standard one, which in lab tests produced about ten times more receptors. At Enveda, it reviewed 1,061 pages of manufacturing records, caught all 11 issues human reviewers found plus seven more, and cut review time from over 25 hours to under three.

Why it matters: These are Anthropic's own results, reported with its partners and not independently reviewed. Most come from laboratory or mouse experiments; Mithril's earliest possible human trial is late 2028, and only if its candidates keep holding up. Several items, such as the Bristol Myers Squibb trial-planning work, have no outcomes yet. The open question is whether general-purpose AI models shorten the path to approved drugs, or mainly speed up the paperwork and logistics around them.

Sources: Anthropic research update · Endpoints News (paywalled) · The State of AI

Protein October 27, 2026

Pythia Labs launches with an AI model that designs complete antibodies from scratch, including against hard-to-drug receptor targets

Pythia Labs launched on October 27 with a preprint reporting that its AI model, Pythia-1, designed complete human antibodies from scratch against 22 drug targets. The targets include GPCRs, a family of cell-surface receptors that antibodies have found hard to target.

The AI does the design. Pythia-1 reasons jointly over an antibody's amino-acid sequence, its three-dimensional atomic structure and design specifications such as where on the target to bind. After one round of de novo design, the company tested about 48 candidates per target in the lab. It reports single-digit nanomolar binding affinity for soluble targets and, for GPCRs, antibodies that either switched the receptor on or blocked it in cell tests.

Why it matters: This is a company-reported preprint, not peer reviewed, and it reports no animal or human data. The claim that the antibodies compare favorably with clinical-stage antibodies does not name the comparators or the measurements in the press release. Pythia says it is releasing the lead antibody sequences, which lets outside laboratories test the claims.

Sources: Preprint on bioRxiv · Pythia Labs press release (Business Wire)

Funding October 26, 2026

Astellas signs a deal worth more than $400 million with AI Proteins to develop computer-designed miniproteins

Astellas, the Japanese drug company, has signed a research and license agreement with AI Proteins, a US biotech, under which AI Proteins is eligible for more than $400 million in upfront and milestone payments, plus royalties on any product sales. The deal covers miniproteins: small proteins built to grip a chosen target.

The AI designs the proteins. AI Proteins uses its AI platform and de novo protein design to create miniproteins on a computer and then optimize their properties. Astellas selects the targets, holds an exclusive worldwide license to the resulting candidates, and would run development and sales.

Why it matters: The $400 million is a ceiling that depends on milestones being reached. The upfront payment, royalty rates, targets and timelines were not disclosed, and the report mentions no clinical data or development stage for any candidate. Claims that miniproteins outperform traditional biologics come from the companies, not independent testing.

Sources: BioSpectrum Asia · Fierce Biotech (headline only; page not retrievable)

Funding October 20, 2026

Lilly signs a deal worth up to $1.1 billion with Hong Kong's METiS TechBio to use machine learning to design drug-delivery particles

Eli Lilly and METiS TechBio, a Hong Kong company, announced a collaboration on October 20 to develop new lipid nanoparticles: tiny fat-based capsules that carry drugs, such as mRNA, into cells. Lilly will pay an upfront amount in the low double-digit millions of dollars. METiS is eligible for up to $1.1 billion in milestone payments, plus single-digit royalties on sales.

The AI optimizes the capsule. METiS's platform, called AiLNP, uses machine learning to discover and tune ionizable lipids, the ingredient that largely determines how well a particle carries its cargo and which tissues it reaches. Lilly wants particles that are more specific to the tissue they target.

Why it matters: The $1.1 billion is a maximum tied to development, regulatory and commercial milestones; the guaranteed upfront payment is much smaller. The partners did not say which Lilly drugs or diseases are involved, gave no timeline, and cited no data showing the platform beats existing delivery particles. Better tissue targeting is the stated goal, not a demonstrated result.

Sources: BioSpace · Fierce Biotech (headline only; page not retrievable)
