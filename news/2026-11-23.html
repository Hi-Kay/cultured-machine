Funding November 17–19, 2026

Isomorphic raises $5 billion, then signs Merck two days later

No drug designed by Isomorphic Labs has ever been given to a patient. On November 17 the London company, spun out of Google DeepMind, raised $5 billion anyway. On November 19, Merck said it would use Isomorphic’s software to design small-molecule drugs against a cancer target it has not named. The round was co-led by Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital, with Alphabet investing again, and takes the company’s total funding past $7.7 billion.

The AI does the design. Isomorphic’s engine, IsoDDE, predicts the 3D shape of a target protein, works out how a candidate molecule would sit in it, and estimates the binding affinity — how tightly the two would hold together. In the Merck agreement, Isomorphic runs that computer work using some of Merck’s own lab data and hands over molecule designs; Merck does all the lab testing, the trials, the manufacturing and the selling. Isomorphic says the money will go to maturing IsoDDE, moving into new areas of biology, and building the infrastructure to run its own trials.

Why it matters: A company that has never given its drugs to a patient just raised more money than many mid-size drugmakers are worth, on the strength of design software alone.

The catch: No Isomorphic drug has entered human testing. Demis Hassabis said in January that the first trials were expected by the end of 2026, and the funding announcement claims positive preclinical results in cancer and immune diseases without publishing any of the data. The Merck deal names no target and no amounts: Isomorphic gets an undisclosed upfront payment, then milestones and royalties if anything works.

Sources: Isomorphic Labs funding announcement (via BIA) · Merck press release (PDF)

Discovery November 20, 2026

An AI designs working RNA from a plain English request

Ask for an RNA molecule in ordinary English and RhoAgent will draft one. When the team behind it, reporting in Nature Biotechnology, built those drafts for real, they worked. Most people meet RNA as a messenger. It does more than that: some RNA molecules cut other molecules, and some glow when they grip a target, which makes them useful as switches and sensors inside a living cell. Designing them from scratch has lagged years behind protein design.

The AI runs the whole loop. An AI agent built on an open language model turns the plain-English goal into a specification; a second model draws a 3D RNA shape and then picks the sequence of letters to match; the designs are then screened for clashes and checked against a structure predictor, and failures are sent back for another round. The published summary reports synthetic ribozymes that worked while sharing only 40–70% of their letters with the natural versions, one cutting RNA up to six times faster than a version trimmed by human experts, and glowing aptamer scaffolds about 230% brighter.

Why it matters: RNA tools are how a lot of modern biology gets measured and controlled, and until now they have been built mostly by hand.

The catch: The figures above come from the paper’s published summary rather than our own reading of the full paper. The authors say the system mainly executes precise instructions and gives unpredictable results from vague ones, that it models a single fixed shape rather than molecules that switch between shapes, and that it works best below about 200 RNA letters.

Sources: Nature Biotechnology paper · Research summary

Funding November 18, 2026

Boltz raises $110 million giving its software away

Boltz gives its main product away, and just raised $110 million. The London company, spun out of MIT, was led in the round by Bessemer Venture Partners. Its main product is not a drug but software: models that predict what a protein and a drug candidate look like locked together, estimate how strongly they hold, and propose new molecules built to grip a chosen target. The core models are published free; the company sells access to faster hosted versions and to models it keeps private.

The AI is the product. Boltz says its free models have been downloaded more than 5 million times and are used by more than 100,000 scientists, including at Pfizer, GSK, Takeda and Bristol Myers Squibb, and that they match AlphaFold 3 at predicting structures. The new money goes to computing power, data and hiring; the team is about 40 researchers.

Why it matters: The kind of structure prediction that was a corporate advantage three years ago is now a free download, and the business has moved to selling speed and private models on top of it.

The catch: The download count, the user count and the comparison with AlphaFold 3 are all the company’s own figures, measured on its own benchmarks. Boltz disclosed no revenue, and no drug designed with its models has been shown to work in a patient.

Sources: Bessemer Venture Partners · Tech.eu

Discovery November 18, 2026

A cancer hospital hands its tumour data to an AI startup

One of the world's best-known cancer hospitals is handing its tumour data to a startup. Memorial Sloan Kettering will let Noetik, a San Francisco company, train AI models on records covering tens of thousands of patients. The aim is to predict which patients a given cancer treatment will actually help — the question oncologists most often cannot answer in advance.

The AI learns from the data. Noetik’s OCTO models are foundation models: single large models trained on many tumour samples at once so they pick up general patterns, rather than one model per question. The companies describe the goal as a world model of cancer biology, meaning a model that can be asked what would happen if a tumour were treated a particular way. MSK supplies the pathology data for training; Noetik supplies the models.

Why it matters: Most cancer drugs help only a minority of the patients who take them, and nobody can reliably say in advance which minority.

The catch: This is an announcement of a collaboration, not a result. No financial terms, duration or prediction accuracy were disclosed, and the release notes that MSK itself holds a financial interest in Noetik.

Sources: Noetik press release (via BioSpace)

Discovery November 20, 2026

Genesis claims faster binding predictions, and shows its training data

Most companies publish the score and keep the exam paper. Genesis Molecular AI released both: a model called Ruby, which predicts potency (how much of a molecule it takes to shut down its target protein), and the training set behind it. The company says Ruby ranks candidates more accurately than the methods it tested against, including physics-based calculations, on four public benchmarks and on eight lab assays from its work with Incyte.

The AI skips a slow step. Earlier approaches build a 3D model of each molecule sitting in its target before scoring it. Ruby reuses the internal representations of the company’s structure-prediction model instead, which it says brings the cost down to 25 milliseconds per molecule at its fastest setting — 40 to more than 1,000 times faster than the other methods it compared. The released dataset, GenesisPotency-2026, is public literature data re-checked by hand; in one case 885 measurements of a protein’s inhibitors had been filed under an unrelated protein in two databases.

Why it matters: Chemistry models are trained on public databases nobody had checked closely, so cleaning up the data may buy as much accuracy as a better model.

The catch: This is a company announcement, and its accuracy claims come with no numbers at all — no correlations, no error rates, no figures for the methods it beat. The technical report has not been peer reviewed, and the release does not say how large the dataset is.

Sources: Genesis Molecular AI press release
