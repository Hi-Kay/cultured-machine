Oncology September 22, 2026

Boehringer Ingelheim bets over $1 billion on an AI platform that finds cancer targets hidden in RNA splicing errors

Boehringer Ingelheim and AI biotech Envisagenics announced a multi-year collaboration worth more than $1 billion in potential payments to find new cancer drug targets hidden in RNA splicing — the process cells use to edit genetic instructions before turning them into proteins. When splicing goes wrong inside a tumor, it can produce a protein fragment that healthy cells never make, and a drug built to recognize that fragment can attack the tumor while leaving normal tissue alone.

The AI does the work through Envisagenics' SpliceCore platform, which has catalogued more than 14 million RNA splicing events and combines machine learning with large-scale transcriptomic data to flag which of those splicing errors are cancer-specific, common enough across patients to matter, and realistic to target with a drug — a filtering task far too large to do by manually inspecting each event. Boehringer will test the AI-flagged targets against several drug formats, including antibody-drug conjugates and T-cell engagers, for hard-to-treat solid tumors.

Why it matters: This is a research and target-validation deal, not a drug — none of the targets Envisagenics has flagged has yet produced a therapy that has entered human trials, and most of Boehringer's payments are tied to milestones that may never be reached. What it does show is that a major pharma company is willing to bet at meaningful scale on an AI-first, splicing-based approach to finding cancer targets, following earlier SpliceCore collaborations Envisagenics struck with Bristol Myers Squibb, Johnson & Johnson, and Biogen.

Sources: GlobeNewswire · Fierce Biotech

Foundation models September 8, 2026

Google DeepMind maps the predicted effect of all 9 billion possible DNA changes in the human genome

Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome Atlas, a free, public catalogue predicting the molecular effect of roughly 9 billion possible single-letter changes across the human genome, with a particular focus on the 98% of DNA that doesn't code for any protein. That non-coding DNA is where genome-wide studies most often turn up disease-linked variants, but it has historically been the hardest part of the genome to interpret, since there is no protein sequence to compare it against.

The AI does the work through AlphaGenome, a genome language model DeepMind built to predict how a DNA sequence change affects nearby gene activity, splicing, and other molecular processes; the new Atlas pre-computes those predictions for every possible variant and condenses them into a single ranking number, the AlphaGenome Variant Impact (AVI) score. Researchers at the Broad Institute used the AVI score to flag a previously overlooked variant in a gene called DNM1 that causes a severe, rare form of childhood epilepsy, then confirmed in the lab that the variant broke the gene's splicing exactly as AlphaGenome had predicted.

Why it matters: The Atlas gives researchers, especially rare-disease teams without funding for large-scale sequencing studies, a shortcut for deciding which of millions of candidate variants are worth testing, rather than a way to test them all. But AVI scores remain predictions, not diagnoses — DeepMind states plainly that AlphaGenome "has not been validated for, and is not approved for, any clinical use," so every flagged variant still needs experimental confirmation before it can inform a patient's care.

Sources: Google DeepMind

Discovery September 21, 2026

AbbVie taps AI drug-design startup Iambic to hunt for new small-molecule medicines

AbbVie signed a multi-year collaboration with Iambic Therapeutics to speed up the discovery of small-molecule drugs across immunology, neuroscience, and oncology, aiming for medicines that are either first of their kind or meaningfully better than what already exists. Small-molecule drug discovery traditionally means testing each candidate compound's properties largely one at a time — how tightly it binds its target, its ADMET profile, its toxicity — a slow, sequential process that limits how many ideas a team can explore.

The AI does the work through Iambic's Enchant v3 model, trained on more than 6,000 molecular properties, which evaluates many of those properties for a candidate molecule at the same time rather than one after another, letting chemists test far more design hypotheses in parallel before committing a compound to the lab. Iambic will receive an upfront payment plus milestone payments and royalties tied to any medicines that eventually reach the market.

Why it matters: Deals like this are a bet on faster, cheaper early-stage discovery, not a finished drug — Iambic's own most advanced program has taken roughly two years to reach the clinic, still just the front end of a drug development process that typically runs a decade or more, and nothing about this partnership shortens the human trials that actually decide whether a molecule works and is safe.

Sources: AbbVie News Center

Funding September 24, 2026

AI biologics startup BigHat raises $75M as its first AI-designed antibody-drug conjugate reaches patients

BigHat Biosciences raised a $75 million Series C, bringing its total funding to $223 million, as the company advances BHB810 — an antibody-drug conjugate for gastric and other gastrointestinal cancers that is now dosing patients in a Phase 1 trial and is among the first AI-designed biologics to reach human testing.

The AI does the work inside what BigHat calls its "integrated learning loop": the company runs its own automated wet-lab experiments to generate large amounts of proprietary antibody data, then feeds that data back to train and improve its in-house AI models for protein design, rather than relying mainly on public datasets the way many rival platforms do. That loop, the company says, lets it design antibodies for specific developability properties — stability, manufacturability — that determine whether a molecule that binds its target in a dish can actually become a medicine.

Why it matters: BHB810 is still in Phase 1, the earliest and highest-attrition stage of human testing, so its AI-designed origin says nothing yet about whether it will prove safe or effective in patients. The bigger signal in this round is the pharmaceutical industry's continued appetite for funding the data-generation side of AI drug design, not just the algorithms — investors here include the venture arms of Amgen, Eli Lilly, and Merck alongside BigHat's existing backers.

Sources: BigHat Biosciences (Business Wire)

Reality check September 8, 2026

FOIA documents show Medicare's AI-driven prior-authorization pilot delayed care and denied thousands of requests

The Electronic Frontier Foundation sued Medicare's Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) for records on WISeR, a pilot program that uses AI to decide whether Medicare will cover certain medical procedures before a doctor performs them, and the roughly 1,000 pages it won show widespread delays, denials, and reports of patient harm since the program launched across six states in January. One tracked request went unanswered for 83 days against a promised 72-hour turnaround, and two of the private companies CMS pays to run the AI review denied nearly 6,000 requests in the program's first three months.

The AI does the work as the first-line reviewer: private vendor companies use AI systems to evaluate each prior-authorization request and recommend approval or denial, with a human clinician required to sign off only on denials — even though outside research on human-AI decision-making shows an AI's initial recommendation often steers the human reviewer's final call. The vendors are paid for requests they deny, not for denials later reversed on appeal, and one vendor, Innovaccer, told CMS a month before launch that it would "auto-affirm" every request at first because its AI software wasn't fully built or tested, rather than ask CMS to delay the start date.

Why it matters: CMS says a qualified clinician reviews every denial and ties vendor payments to quality scores meant to discourage bad calls, but the same documents show a low score cuts a vendor's payment by only 5 to 10% — a thin penalty next to the financial reward for denying care. The episode is a concrete case study in a problem bigger than Medicare: an AI system deployed to speed up a bureaucratic decision can create new incentives and failure modes of its own that stay invisible until the paperwork is forced into the open.

Sources: Electronic Frontier Foundation
