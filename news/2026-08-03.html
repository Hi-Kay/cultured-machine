Protein design July 22, 2026

Scientists combined AI-designed proteins with lab evolution — and built an enzyme 79 times better at its new job

Researchers at the Broad Institute, led by gene-editing pioneer David Liu, showed that giving lab evolution an AI-designed starting point produces far better proteins than evolving a natural one from scratch. They used an AI model to redesign botulinum neurotoxin protease — the enzyme behind Botox, which normally cuts a completely different target — into a more stable version of itself, without changing its 3D shape.

That extra stability turned out to matter enormously. The team then used PACE, a rapid lab-evolution method Liu's group invented in 2011, to evolve the AI-stabilized protein to cut ataxin-2, a protein that clumps together and drives neurodegeneration when it misfolds. The AI-redesigned starting point evolved into an enzyme 79 times better at cutting ataxin-2 than versions evolved from the natural, non-redesigned protease — because a more stable protein can absorb more mutations before it breaks.

Why it matters: This is a lab proof-of-concept, not a therapy — the engineered enzyme hasn't been tested in animals or people, and cutting ataxin-2 in a dish is a long way from treating neurodegeneration. But the underlying finding is general: neither AI design nor lab evolution alone got as far as combining them, and the team says the same trick should work for many other proteins, including ones used in gene-editing tools.

Sources: Broad Institute · Nature (DOI)

Protein design July 16, 2026

Nobel laureate David Baker's lab designed proteins from scratch that light up specific molecules inside living cells

A team spanning the Baker Lab, Janelia Research Campus, and EMBL Heidelberg built a new class of microscopy tools called NovoTags: small proteins designed from scratch by AI to grip a bright synthetic dye tightly and specifically, so scientists can genetically attach the tag to any protein of interest and see exactly where it sits inside a living human cell. The AI pipeline used AlphaFold and related structure-prediction tools to filter candidate designs before anything was made in the lab.

The tags come in three colors that can be used together, and a follow-on version called NovoSplit only assembles — and lights up — once a specific dye is added, acting as a chemically controlled on/off switch for studying when two proteins interact. Combined with additional imaging tricks, the system could in principle track up to 30 different proteins in the same cell at once.

Why it matters: This is a laboratory instrument, not a medicine — its direct beneficiaries are researchers studying cell biology, not patients. But better ways to see what's happening inside a cell are exactly the kind of tool that feeds the next round of AI-driven discovery, including future drug-target research.

Sources: EMBL · Science (DOI)

Oncology July 28, 2026

AI-guided imaging found cancer-linked changes in pancreatic cells that still looked normal under the microscope

A research team spanning MD Anderson and the Novo Nordisk Foundation Center for Protein Research mapped the molecular changes that happen years before pancreatic cancer forms — one of the deadliest cancers, usually caught too late for surgery to help. An AI model trained to read microscope images automatically classified tissue into normal ducts, early pre-cancerous changes, and more advanced precursor lesions, across samples from both cancer-free organ donors and pancreatic cancer patients.

That AI classification told the researchers exactly which tiny clusters of cells — as few as 100 — to physically cut out with a laser for ultra-sensitive mass spectrometry, which measured over 8,500 proteins in each sample. The result: cancer-associated changes in stress response, immune activity, metabolism, and mitochondrial function showed up in duct cells that looked completely normal under the microscope, before any visible precancerous change had occurred.

Why it matters: This is a discovery atlas, not a diagnostic test — it identifies candidate biomarkers rather than something a doctor can order today. But it makes a concrete case that molecular changes precede what pathologists can currently see, which is exactly the gap earlier-detection tools would need to close for a cancer this lethal.

Sources: AACR · Cancer Discovery

Reality check July 17, 2026

How are AI-designed drugs actually doing in human trials? A genuinely mixed scorecard

Insilico Medicine's rentosertib — whose target was identified by an AI biology model before AI generative chemistry designed the molecule — moved into a 52-week Phase 3 trial for a lung-scarring disease called idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, after a small Phase 2 study showed it improved lung function. It's one of the furthest-advanced AI-originated drug candidates in the world, though it still hasn't proven itself in the larger trial. Insilico also has a second AI-flagged candidate, for ulcerative colitis, now in Phase 2.

Recursion Pharmaceuticals offers a more mixed picture: after disappointing results forced it to prune two other AI-derived programs earlier this year, its drug REC-4881 — whose mechanism AI flagged as promising — produced a 43% median reduction in colorectal polyp burden in an early trial for a rare hereditary condition, with the effect holding up for weeks after patients stopped taking it. Meanwhile Verge Genomics's AI-identified ALS drug candidate failed to advance past its first human trial.

Why it matters: AI-discovered drugs tend to do somewhat better than average in Phase 1 safety trials, but that edge doesn't clearly survive into the larger trials that actually prove a drug works — and no AI-originated drug has yet won regulatory approval. The honest read: AI is generating real, testable leads faster, but turning a lead into an approved medicine still runs into the same biology and trial risk it always has.

Sources: BioPharma Dive · Insilico Medicine
