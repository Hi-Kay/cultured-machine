How AlphaFold predicts a protein's shape

~5 min read · 3 things to play with · Updated November 2026

Every two years since 1994, a few hundred scientists enter a competition almost nobody can win. For 26 years, nobody did.

The contest nobody could win

The rules are brutal and simple. Somewhere in the world, a lab has just worked out the exact 3D shape of a protein nobody has ever mapped before. The organisers lock that answer in a drawer. Then they hand out the protein's recipe and say: tell us what it looks like.

It is called CASP, and a biologist named John Moult started it because the field had a credibility problem. Everyone claimed their prediction software worked. Nobody could prove it, because everyone tested on proteins whose answers were already published. Moult's fix was to make the answers secret.

The scores were humbling. By 2014, after twenty years of effort, they had stopped improving at all.

Then in 2020 a team from a London AI lab entered and scored 92 out of 100. That is the point where a prediction is about as good as measuring the thing in a laboratory. Moult, who had spent twenty-six years watching clever people fail at this, told Nature: "In some sense the problem is solved."

Here is what they had solved.

What they were guessing

Picture a necklace with beads in twenty colours. That is a protein. The beads are amino acids, and your DNA is the list that says which bead comes next.

The necklace does not stay a necklace. The instant a cell builds it, the thing crumples in on itself and settles into one specific, intricate, reproducible shape. It does this in less than a second, every time, without being told how.

From string to shape

Ten beads, one chain. Same beads before and after. Only the shape changes.

Right now it is just a floppy string. It can't do anything yet.

That shape is the entire point. A key opens a door because of its shape, and a protein works the same way. One shape carries oxygen around your blood. A different one is the spike the flu virus uses to break into your cells.

Reading the beads is easy. We have been able to do it cheaply for decades. Finding the shape meant months of laboratory work, sometimes years, for a single protein, which is why a PhD student could spend most of a degree on one.

Why guessing is hard

So why not let a computer try every possible shape and keep the best one? Pull the slider below and watch what happens to the number.

A real protein runs to hundreds of beads. Checking every shape would take longer than the universe has been around, and the protein itself does it in a fraction of a second, which is either a miracle or a hint that brute force is the wrong idea. A biologist called Cyrus Levinthal pointed this out in 1969 and people have been calling it a paradox ever since.

It is less a paradox than a clue. Whatever the protein is doing down there, it is not searching.

The clue hiding in a fish

You and a trout share a lot of proteins. Not identical ones — each species has been collecting its own small spelling changes for a few hundred million years — but close enough to line up side by side.

Do that for enough species and something odd shows up: certain beads change together. Below is a simplified version, with two of the ten columns in on a secret. See if you can find them before you press the button.

One protein, six species

Each row is the same stretch of protein in a different species. Each letter is one bead.

12345678910

Hint: look at columns 3 and 8.

Column 3 and column 8 sit five beads apart on the string, so why would they care about each other?

Because once the thing is folded, they are touching. One carries a positive charge and the other a negative one, and they hold on to each other like two magnets. Evolution will happily flip one of them — but only if the other flips too. Any animal that got one mutation without the other had a broken protein and did not pass it on.

Far apart on the string, neighbours in the fold

The same ten beads, with 3 and 8 coloured in.

Five beads apart, and nothing in common.

Which means that every pair of beads that touch in the final shape has been leaving a trail in the fossil record of DNA for a billion years, waiting for somebody to notice.

Beads that change together sit together. Collect enough pairs and you have a map of the shape.

Putting it together

AlphaFold, built at Google DeepMind, turns that into a three-step recipe.

📚Collect the family album

Find your protein in thousands of other species and line them all up.

🧲Spot the pairs

Work out which beads change together. Those are probably touching.

🧩Build and polish

Assemble a shape that fits every clue, then refine it over and over.

The engine is a neural network, the same family of AI behind chatbots, trained on every protein shape labs had already measured. So it is not reasoning from physics. It has simply seen enough real proteins to know what one looks like, the way you can tell a real face from a drawing of one without being able to explain how.

What happened next

DeepMind could have sold it. Instead, in 2022, they ran the model over almost every protein known to science and put the results on the internet for anyone.

Yes, the dot on the left is the lab square. Not all predictions are equally reliable: about a third are rated highly accurate.

A problem that had been open since 1972, when Christian Anfinsen stood up at the Nobel ceremony and argued that a protein's shape must be written somewhere in its sequence, had turned into a free lookup. In 2024 the Nobel committee came back around and gave the chemistry prize to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for it.

Why it matters: Anyone designing a medicine or chasing down a genetic disease can now pull up a protein's shape in seconds instead of spending years measuring it.

Where it still gets stuck

Proteins move. AlphaFold hands you a photograph. Plenty of proteins do their job by flexing, snapping shut, springing open — and predicting that film is still largely unsolved.

It needs the family album. A protein with few known relatives gives it fewer clues, which is awkward, because the fastest-changing proteins include the viral and immune ones we most want to understand.

It is still a guess. A very good one. The lab still gets the last word.

AlphaFold 3, released in 2024, closed one of the bigger gaps: it can now predict a protein together with whatever it grabs hold of, including a drug molecule or a strand of DNA.

None of which changes the shape of the achievement. A question that cost a laboratory years now costs a researcher an afternoon, and the people who answered it gave the answers away.
