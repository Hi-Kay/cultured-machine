How AlphaFold predicts a protein's shape

~5 min read · 3 things to play with · Updated November 2026

Your body runs on billions of tiny machines called proteins. Each one starts life as a floppy string, then folds itself into a shape. For 50 years, nobody could predict that shape. Then an AI did.

The whole story in one sentence: AlphaFold looks at how a protein has changed across thousands of species, works out which parts must be touching, and builds the 3D shape from those clues.

1. A protein is a string that folds itself

Think of a necklace with beads in 20 different colours. That is a protein. The beads are called amino acids, and your DNA is the instruction list that says which bead comes next.

But the necklace does not stay a necklace. As soon as it is made, it crumples into a very exact 3D shape, usually in less than a second. Press the button and watch.

From string to shape

Ten beads, one chain. Same beads before and after. Only the shape changes.

Right now it is just a floppy string. It can't do anything yet.

The shape is the whole point. A key only opens a door because of its shape. A protein is the same. One shape carries oxygen in your blood. Another digests your lunch. Another is the spike a virus uses to break into your cells.

So if you want to understand a disease or design a medicine, you need the shape.

2. Why you can't just guess

Reading the order of the beads is easy and cheap. Finding the shape used to mean months or years of lab work for a single protein.

Why not let a computer try every possible shape? Because there are far too many. Drag the slider and see how fast it gets out of hand.

How many ways can a chain fold?

Every extra bead multiplies the number of possible shapes by about three.

Real proteins are often hundreds of beads long. Checking every shape would take longer than the universe has existed. Yet the protein itself finds the right one almost instantly. Scientists call this puzzle Levinthal's paradox, and it is why brute force was never going to work.

3. The trick: evolution already left the answers lying around

Here is the clever part. You have the same proteins as a mouse, a fish and even a bacterium. Not identical, but close. Over millions of years, each species collected its own small spelling changes.

Line up those versions and something odd shows up. Some beads change together. Below is a toy example. Can you spot the two positions that always change as a pair?

One protein, six species

Each row is the same stretch of protein in a different species. Each letter is one bead.

12345678910

Hint: look at columns 3 and 8.

Why would two beads far apart on the string care about each other? Because in the folded shape they are touching. One carries a plus charge, the other a minus, and they hold on to each other like two magnets. If one flips, the other has to flip too, or the protein falls apart.

Far apart on the string, neighbours in the fold

The same ten beads, with 3 and 8 coloured in.

Beads 3 and 8 are five beads apart.

Beads that change together sit together. Find enough of these pairs and you have a map of the shape.

4. What AlphaFold actually does

AlphaFold, built by Google DeepMind, turns that idea into a recipe with three steps.

📚Collect the family album

Find versions of your protein in thousands of other species and line them up.

🧲Spot the pairs

Work out which beads change together. Those are probably touching.

🧩Build and polish

Assemble a 3D shape that fits all the clues, then refine it again and again.

The engine doing this is a neural network. It is the same family of AI that powers chatbots, but trained on the protein shapes scientists had already measured in the lab. It learned what real proteins look like, so it can make a very good guess for a new one.

It is a bit like a 3D jigsaw where evolution has quietly told you which pieces share an edge.

5. How big a deal was it?

In 2020, AlphaFold's predictions came close to the accuracy of lab experiments for many proteins, in minutes instead of months. Two years later the team published predicted shapes for nearly every protein known to science, free for anyone.

50 years of lab work vs. one AI

Each square's area shows how many protein shapes were available.

~200,000measured in labs over about 50 years
200 million+predicted by AlphaFold

Yes, the dot on the left is the lab square. Not all predictions are equally reliable: about a third are rated highly accurate.

In 2024, AlphaFold's creators Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it.

Why it matters: Scientists who want to design a medicine or understand a disease can now look up a protein's shape in seconds instead of spending years measuring it.

6. What it still can't do

  • Proteins move. AlphaFold gives you a photo, not a film. Many proteins bend and snap between shapes as they work. Predicting that movement is still hard.
  • It needs the family album. Proteins with few relatives, or ones that change very fast like some virus and immune proteins, give it fewer clues.
  • A prediction is still a prediction. For anything important, the lab has the final word.

The newer AlphaFold 3 (2024) fixed one big gap: it can predict a protein together with the thing it grabs, such as a drug or a piece of DNA.
