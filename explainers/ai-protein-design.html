How AI invents a protein that never existed

~5 min read · 2 things to play with · Updated November 2026

Evolution has been writing proteins for four billion years and has barely started. Take a short one, a hundred beads long. The number of ways to spell it is a number with 130 digits. Every protein in every living thing on Earth is a rounding error against that.

So the entire history of life on this planet has explored almost none of what is possible. Everything that could exist, and does not, is sitting out there unwritten.

People are now writing some of it.

Make a key for a lock

A lot of modern medicine is one protein grabbing hold of one other thing and refusing to let go. An antibody clamping onto a virus. A blocker plugging a receptor on a cancer cell so the signal telling it to grow never arrives.

The target is a lock and the medicine is a key, and shape decides whether it turns.

For a century we found keys by searching. Inject a mouse, wait, harvest whatever antibodies it made. Or screen millions of random candidates and hope. It works — most biologic drugs on the market were found roughly this way — but you get what nature hands you, which is not necessarily what you asked for.

The new idea: stop searching for the key. Draw it.

Step one: dream

You have probably seen an AI image generator turn a field of static into a photograph of a dog. Protein designers borrowed the same machinery, called diffusion, and pointed it at molecules.

It starts with beads scattered at random beside the target. Then it tidies, a little at a time, until they have arranged themselves into something that grips. Press play.

From noise to a new protein

Grey blob: the target. Coloured beads: the new protein. Drag the slider, or press play.

Pure noise. No protein yet.

How does it know what a protein is meant to look like? It practised. Researchers took thousands of real structures, wrecked them with noise, and trained the model to put them back together. Do that enough times and it can start from nothing but static and still land on something protein-shaped.

Press "new random start" a few times. Different static, different design. That turns out to be the useful part: you can ask for ten thousand ideas and get ten thousand genuinely different ones.

Step two: write the recipe

A shape on a screen is not a protein. A cell can only build from a list of beads in order, so a second model answers the reverse question: which string of amino acids would fold into this?

If you read the AlphaFold explainer, this is that puzzle running backwards. AlphaFold goes string to shape. This goes shape to string.

Step three: mark your own homework

Now a rather elegant trick. Take the new recipe, hand it to AlphaFold, say nothing about what you were aiming for, and see whether it folds back into the shape you drew.

If yes, keep it. If no, the recipe probably folds into something else entirely and you throw it away. This costs almost nothing, so teams run it across thousands of designs and keep a handful.

🔍Check

🧪Test

Make it for real and see if it sticks.

Then reality turns up

Everything so far happened on a screen, where things are cheap and nothing can go wrong. Somebody now has to grow the protein, purify it, and measure whether it actually grabs the target. This is slow, costs real money, and is where the attrition happens.

A model called Pinal was asked to design a protein that glows. Watch what survives.

One out of fifty thousand looks like a failure, and for a lot of purposes it is. But that one protein had never existed, anywhere, and somebody got it by typing a sentence. On easier jobs the odds are far better — in the same study, five of twenty designs worked for a different task. The honest summary is that this works, badly, and is improving quickly.

Why it matters: Instead of hunting through nature for a protein that happens to fit, you can now order one built to measure and find out within weeks whether it works.

Where it still gets stuck

Sticking is not healing. A protein that grabs its target is a starting point, not a drug. It still has to survive in a body, reach the right place, avoid setting off the immune system, and help someone. Most candidates die somewhere in there.

The computer check is not proof. Designs that look flawless on screen fail in a tube all the time, and nobody can reliably predict which.

Moving targets are brutal. If the lock wobbles or changes shape depending on what it is doing, designing a key for it gets much harder.

David Baker shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this line of work, alongside the two people who built AlphaFold. The prize was for proving that proteins can be designed to order at all. Turning that proof into something a drug company can rely on is the part everyone is still working on.
