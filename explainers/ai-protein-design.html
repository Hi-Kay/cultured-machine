How AI invents a protein that never existed

~5 min read · 2 things to play with · Published November 2026

For most of history, we could only use the proteins nature happened to make. Now a computer can draw a brand-new one, made to order, in an afternoon. Here is how that works, and why most of the drawings still end up in the bin.

The whole story in one sentence: The AI dreams up a shape that fits the target, writes the recipe for it, checks its own work, and then the lab finds out if it was right.

1. The goal: make a key for a lock

Many medicines are proteins that stick to one exact thing in your body and nothing else. An antibody that grabs a virus. A blocker that plugs a receptor on a cancer cell.

Think of the target as a lock. The medicine is the key. The shape has to fit.

The old way to find a key was to search. Inject an animal and harvest the antibodies it makes, or test millions of random candidates and hope one fits. It works, but it is slow, and you get what you find, not what you want.

The new way: don't search for the key. Draw it.

2. Step one: dream up a shape

You may have seen AI image generators turn a cloud of random dots into a picture of a cat. Protein design tools use the same trick, called diffusion.

The AI starts with beads scattered at random next to the target. Then it tidies them up, a little at a time, until they form a protein that hugs the target. Try it.

From noise to a new protein

Grey blob: the target. Coloured beads: the new protein. Drag the slider, or press play.

Pure noise. No protein yet.

How does it know what a protein should look like? It practised. Researchers took thousands of real protein shapes, scrambled them with noise, and trained the AI to un-scramble them. After enough practice it can start from pure noise and still end up with something protein-like.

Press "New random start" a few times. Different noise gives a different design. That is useful: you can ask for ten thousand ideas and get ten thousand different ones.

3. Step two: write the recipe

A shape on a screen is not a protein yet. A cell can only build a protein from a list of beads (amino acids) in the right order. So a second AI answers the question: which string of beads will fold into this shape?

If you read the AlphaFold explainer, this is the same puzzle run backwards. AlphaFold goes from string to shape. This step goes from shape to string.

4. Step three: check your own homework

Now comes a neat trick. Take the new recipe and hand it to AlphaFold, without telling it what shape you wanted. Does it predict the same shape you drew?

  • Yes: good sign. Keep it.
  • No: the recipe probably folds into something else. Throw it away.

This filter is cheap, so teams run it on thousands of designs and keep only the best few.

5. Step four: the lab has the last word

Everything so far happened on a computer. Now someone has to make the protein for real and measure whether it sticks. This is the slow, expensive part, and it is where most designs fail.

Here is a real example from a recent digest. A model called Pinal was asked to design a protein that glows.

One winner out of 50,000 ideas sounds bad. But that one protein did not exist anywhere in nature, and somebody got it by typing a request. The odds depend on the job: in the same study, 5 out of 20 designs worked for a different task. Still, most designs fail.

Why it matters: Instead of spending months searching nature for a protein that happens to fit, scientists can now order one made to measure and test it within weeks.

6. What it still can't do

  • Sticking is not healing. A protein that grabs its target is a starting point. It still has to be safe, last long enough in the body, and actually help patients. That takes years of testing.
  • The computer check is not proof. Plenty of designs look perfect on screen and do nothing in the lab.
  • Moving targets are hard. Locks that wobble or change shape are much tougher to design a key for.

The idea of designing proteins on a computer earned David Baker a share of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, alongside the makers of AlphaFold.
