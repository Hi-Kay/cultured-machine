What is a protein language model?

~6 min read · 2 things to play with · Updated November 2026

Nobody taught this thing any biology. No chemistry lessons, no physics, no textbook, no idea what a disease is. It played fill-in-the-blank a few billion times. Now it can look at a single typo in your DNA and tell you it will probably make you ill.

Life has an alphabet, and we have the complete works

Every protein is written in twenty letters. A, C, D, E, F and so on, one per amino acid. A real one reads something like M K T A Y I A K Q R… and runs for hundreds of letters.

Evolution has been writing in this alphabet since before there were cells, and over the last thirty years we have transcribed hundreds of millions of its sentences into databases. An enormous library, in a language nobody speaks.

That is exactly the situation in which a language model is dangerous.

The game

Take a real protein, cover one letter, and ask the model what is underneath. Then show it the answer.

That is the whole training procedure. It is also, more or less, how ChatGPT learned that "the cat sat on the ___" ends in "mat" rather than "helicopter". Nobody explains the rules of English to it. It plays the game until the rules are the only way to keep winning.

Look at what just happened. The model is not looking anything up. From the neighbours alone it formed an opinion, and usually a narrow one. It will back a small group of chemically similar letters, because at that spot in a real protein only those would do.

What it worked out on its own

To get good at the game, it had no choice but to learn things nobody mentioned:

  • Chemistry. Some positions are buried in an oily core and must stay water-avoiding. Others sit on the wet surface and must stay charged. It figured out which is which.
  • The grammar of folding. Certain runs of letters coil into spirals, others flatten into sheets. It learned the phrasebook.
  • What is forbidden. Most changes would wreck a protein, and it knows which ones a living thing would never tolerate.

It also gives every protein a fingerprint — a list of numbers called an embedding. Two proteins doing the same job land in the same neighbourhood even when their letters barely match. That fingerprint is where most of the usefulness comes from.

Two different questions: AlphaFold asks what shape a sequence folds into. A language model asks whether evolution would ever have written that sequence in the first place. One knows what fits in space. The other knows what looks real. The strongest tools today run both.

What you get for free

Once something knows what "normal" looks like, useful things fall out without anyone designing them.

Judging a mutation. Change one letter and ask: does this still read like a real protein? A confident no is a serious warning sign, which is how a model trained purely on sequences ends up flagging disease-causing variants in a person's genome, without a lab, a patient, or any notion of what illness is.

Writing new proteins. Run it forwards instead and it will draft sequences that read like real ones: the starting point for designing proteins from scratch. Pair that with a structure predictor and you have the machinery behind most AI protein design today.

Searching by meaning. Because every protein has a fingerprint, you can find ones that do a similar job even when their spelling has drifted beyond recognition. Older text-matching search walks straight past these.

Seen in the news: the open-source Germinal antibody designer pairs a structure predictor with a language model trained on real antibodies. The language model's job is to make sure a designed antibody does not merely fold correctly but reads like something an immune system would plausibly have produced.

Where it still gets stuck

It only knows what it has read. Proteins from small families are harder, and the fastest-changing ones (viral, immune) are exactly where the examples run thin and exactly where we need the help.

Natural is not the same as useful. A sequence can look utterly convincing and do nothing you wanted.

It inherits the library's blind spots. Our databases over-represent organisms that are easy to grow and scientists who got funded. The model absorbs all of that without knowing it has.

Why it matters: A model that did nothing but guess covered-up letters can now flag mutations that cause disease, draft new proteins, and find relatives that spelling alone would never reveal.
